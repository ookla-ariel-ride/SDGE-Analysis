Dispatch policy: serve every import priced above the battery's stored-energy cost modeled

A stored kWh costs ~8.4¢ when it comes from midday solar surplus (the forgone export credit is only 7.6¢, ÷ 90% round-trip) or ~13.9¢ from a super-off-peak grid top-up. Against that, the tariff prices imports at 61–87¢ on-peak (4–9pm) and ~51¢ in every off-peak hour — weekday 6–10am, 2–4pm, and 9pm–midnight. So the economically correct policy is not "cover the evening peak": it's discharge against every import outside the super-off-peak windows, and the battery has the energy to do it — average midday surplus (~27 kWh/day) exceeds even the expanded pack's capacity, so morning discharge refills from the sun before the evening peak on most days. Only cloudy winter days bind.

One deliberate exclusion: intervals drawing ≥2.5 kW outside on-peak are EV-charging spillover, and the battery does not serve them. That load moves to pre-6am super-off-peak for free via the charging-schedule fix (§7 LOW) — spending $14,500 battery cycles on load a settings change eliminates would double-pay for the same fix. That's also why the chart above still shows 6–9am imports with a battery: what remains there is EV spillover awaiting the schedule fix, not house load.

8 · Array upgrades: more panels, bigger panels, or new microinverters? No, no, and not yet.

More panels: the numbers say no. The constraint isn't generation — it's timing. The array already exports 60% of what it makes (9,964 kWh/yr) because production peaks midday while the EV charges at night, and since the 2026 TOU change those midday exports credit at only ~10.4¢/kWh. A marginal new-panel kWh therefore earns ~10–12¢, while the marginal import it would offset (overnight super-off-peak) costs just 12.5¢ — nearly a wash. At typical retrofit pricing (~$2.50–3.00/W), added capacity returns roughly $170–190 per kW per year: a 13–16 year payback. A battery is the better use of the same sunlight — it monetizes those midday kWh at 60–87¢ instead of 10¢, and adds backup value.

The regulatory landmine matters more than the math. SDG&E's own NEM tariff (Schedule NEM, Sheet 15, Special Condition 7(b), per Decision D.16-04-020) caps a NEM 2.0 system's growth at the greater of 10% of its capacity "at the time the customer receives the ATO letter" or 1 kW — for this array's 10.05 kW DC nameplate, that's ~1.0 kW (10% and the flat 1 kW floor land almost on top of each other at this size). Exceed it, and the addition — or the whole array, your choice under the tariff — moves onto today's Solar Billing Plan (NBT), where exports earn avoided-cost rates worth a fraction of retail (§13: real hourly rates average ~4.7¢/kWh realized against this home's own export shape, not a flat guess). Your NEM 2.0 status is one of this home's most valuable energy assets, worth $2,103.58–$2,455.64/yr at today's rates (§13) — don't disturb it for low-value midday kWh.

Repowering with higher-capacity panels: also no. That trade only makes sense for failing or badly shaded arrays. Yours produces 1,642 kWh/kW/yr with a rock-stable inverter-vs-meter ratio and no degradation signal at year 6 — you'd be discarding healthy hardware to make more of the energy you already can't use at full value.

Upgrading the microinverters because of clipping: the data says it isn't costing you much. The concern is reasonable: IQ7X units cap at ~315 VA (9.45 kW AC across 30), and Enlighten's per-panel clipping flags look alarming. The 5-minute data says otherwise. On May 11 — a 62.9 kWh peak-production spring day, i.e. maximum clipping risk (cool, clear, high irradiance) — true 5-minute power (PVOutput donation API) topped out at 8,520 W at solar noon against the ~9,450 W AC ceiling: a ~10% headroom cushion with no flat-line at the inverter limit. The annual data agrees: the 99.9th-percentile production hour is 8.82 kWh, and zero days show the flat-topped plateau sustained clipping produces. Whatever per-panel flags Enlighten raises are momentary and energetically trivial (≤1–2% of production, ≤~300 kWh). It's also midday energy worth only ~10.4¢/kWh, and the battery's charging needs are already covered many times over by the 27 kWh/day surplus. Net recoverable value ≤ ~$35/yr against ~$8,000–11,000 to swap 30 microinverters: a non-starter. (May 11's 62.9 kWh daily total is committed in data/pvoutput_daily.csv; its 5-minute curve comes from the PVOutput donation API, and the 8,520 W peak and 8.82 kWh 99.9th-percentile hour are not artifact-backed — the sample file data/pvoutput_5min_sample.csv shows a different day with the same no-clipping signature.) The right time for IQ8s is when IQ7X units start failing — and yours are warrantied through January 2045, so failures get replaced free until then.

When would expansion make sense? If overnight consumption grows structurally — a second EV, electrified heating — and you've already installed storage to move midday energy into that demand; or when bidirectional EV charging (V2H) arrives in your ecosystem, at which point your car's 60–100 kWh pack dwarfs any wall battery and changes the whole calculus. Cheaper interim moves that soak up the 10¢ midday surplus: a heat-pump water heater on a 10am–2pm timer, midday EV top-ups on home days, and pre-cooling.

Level escalation vs spread escalation — the gap is not measurable from this corpus estimated · 4 winter rate changes

The ladder above raises every price by a single rate, which assumes the distance between them holds. What a battery earns is the difference between the on-peak price it displaces and the super-off-peak price it charged at, so the level and the gap are two separate questions. Prices can climb together and leave the gap where it was. data/bill_tou_detail.csv prints both halves on every statement, so the gap is observable: 158 priced cells across 26 billing periods on 25 statements, 2024-05-28 to 2026-06-11. The answer this corpus gives is not determined, for both seasons.

Each point is one rate segment, dated at that segment's midpoint. A billing period splits into two segments when a tariff changes mid-cycle, and dating each at its midpoint keeps a rate that ran a week from counting like one that ran a month. Dashed spans precede each season's largest single step.

Winter looks like it is widening, and the appearance does not survive its own test. After the $0.0286 step of 2025-02-14, the winter spread rises at 10.95%/yr with a 95% interval of 1.89% to 20.81%, which excludes zero. That interval is computed on the window that begins at the largest step in the series, and the largest step was picked by looking at the series. Paying for that choice across the four steps that could have been picked widens the interval to −6.90% to +32.22%, which includes zero. Four independent rate changes cannot both locate a break and estimate a slope through it.

Summer: not determined, and more clearly so. The full-window slope looks strong, and it is one step: $0.0484/kWh on 2025-06-11, 21.4% of the level before it. After that step the slope is 0.5%/yr (95% CI −13.22% to +16.39%, widening to −44.19% to +80.98% once the breakpoint choice is paid for), across only three remaining rate changes.

Two things make the evidence thinner than the bill count suggests. A tariff reprinted unchanged on next month's statement is not a second observation of the price: winter's post-break window prints 15 statement segments carrying 4 distinct spreads, and fitting all 15 would report a 95% interval about a fifth as wide, bought entirely by counting reprints. And a break chosen from the data cannot then be tested against the same data at face value. Both corrections can only ever suppress a claim, never create one, which is why applying them after seeing the first result is not tuning the test toward an answer.

So the battery keeps the assumed ladder, not a measured one. No per-period escalation is published, and the 3/5/8/12% table above stands as the sensitivity it always was. The gap may well be widening. What this corpus cannot do is tell a widening gap apart from a one-off redesign followed by two years of noise. What would settle it, in order of how soon it arrives: more rate changes rather than more statements, since at roughly two delivery redesigns a year a corpus reaching into 2028 would about double the independent units; a breakpoint fixed in advance from published tariff effective dates, which removes the selection penalty entirely; and statements from before 2024-05-25, which would let the two segments be compared as a formal change-point model instead of a refit.

Each observation is one rate segment dated at its midpoint (a period splits when a tariff changes mid-cycle), so a rate in force for a week is not weighted like one in force for a month. Rows priced at $0.00000 are excluded as NEM export-settlement deferrals rather than prices; the script asserts that every such row carries kWh ≤ 0 and aborts if one does not, so a genuine zero price cannot be dropped silently. Adequacy rule, fixed before fitting: a cell needs ≥3 distinct rate vintages and ≥365 days. A spread claim needs all four of: both its cells adequate; the full-window 95% CI excludes zero; the refit on the window after the largest single step agrees in sign and is itself adequate; and that post-break CI still excludes zero after being widened for the fact that the breakpoint was chosen from the data. The last two decide the answer on this corpus: winter clears the first two bars and fails the fourth. Inference is fitted on distinct rate changes rather than statement prints, and on ln(spread), so the rate that gets compounded is the rate that was estimated. Each of these bars can suppress a claim but never create one. Delivery component only; the section gives the reason generation cannot join the series.
