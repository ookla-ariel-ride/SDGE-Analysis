Purpose. This report is a decision document for one household: given a full year of measured energy data, what should this home do about its rate plan, its behavior, storage, and its solar array? Every conclusion is computed from data (meter intervals, parsed bills, production records, and public rate and grid datasets) by committed scripts; none of it is asserted from intuition or vendor claims.   2. Does adding a battery change which plan is best? (§4)

Four behaviors drive the current bill, and the packages in §7 attack them in order of cost:

  • Large loads run during 4–9pm on-peak: 2,568 kWh/yr of >2.5 kW draw (EV top-ups, pool/laundry-class loads) lands in the most expensive window (61–87¢). Moving it past midnight turns ~$1,850 of cost into ~$325.
  • Charging spills past 6am: ~1,900 kWh/yr of high-power draw at 6–9am; past 6am weekdays it pays ~51¢ instead of 12.5¢. Finish charging by 6am.
  • Evening A/C through peak hours: summer evenings average 3–4 kW at 6–9pm. Pre-cooling in the 10am–2pm super-off-peak window (often on your own solar) and easing the setpoint 4–9pm shifts this; physically re-billing a 25% shift of on-peak house load is worth ≈ $480/yr (see §7 LOW; the weather regression in §9 attributes ~$230 of that to cooling specifically).
  • Midday exports earn little: since the 2026 TOU change, 10am–2pm weekday exports are worth only ~10.4¢, the netted rate; the lower surplus credit does not apply here (§8). Self-consuming (laundry, dishwasher, EV top-up, pre-cool) beats exporting-then-reimporting at 51–87¢.

Dispatch policy: price-aware beats evening-only, measured against the bills modeled

A stored kWh costs 11.7¢ when it comes from midday solar surplus, or 14.0¢ from a super-off-peak grid top-up. The imports that energy can displace cost 61–87¢ on-peak (4–9pm) and ~51¢ in every off-peak hour (weekday 6–10am, 2–4pm, and 9pm–midnight). That gap motivates a wider dispatch; what establishes it is re-billing each policy's whole modified year, because the cost of stored energy depends on when it was stored, which an average would hide.

So the better policy is wider than "cover the evening peak": discharge against every import outside the super-off-peak windows, which is what earns the figures in the table below. It is a heuristic the bills reward, with no proof that it is the optimum: it stores surplus whenever it can, without weighing what that surplus was worth where it came from. The battery has the energy to do it: average midday surplus (~27 kWh/day) exceeds even the expanded pack's capacity, so morning discharge refills from the sun before the evening peak on most days. Only cloudy winter days bind.

Both figures are priced through analysis/rates.py from what this dispatch's charging intervals give up; no rate card supplies them. A midday exported kWh cancels an import inside its own month and TOU period (every super-off-peak bucket runs net-import in all 13 months, because that bucket carries overnight EV charging as well as midday sun), so it is worth the netted rate (~10.5¢), whereas a true surplus export is paid 7.6¢; ÷ 90% round-trip gives 11.7¢. A grid-charged kWh also pays the non-bypassable charge, which is billed on gross imports; a forgone export never reduces those, so it never carries it.

Midday is only 47.6% of the surplus this battery stores. Half of it displaces morning and afternoon shoulder exports worth 46–50¢, and 2.4% displaces on-peak exports; averaged over everything the dispatch stores from the sun, a stored kWh costs 34.3¢. Midday-charged energy at 11.7¢ clears every import in this tariff. Shoulder-charged energy at 53.5¢ clears an on-peak import at 60.5–86.8¢ but not an off-peak one at 51.1–51.9¢. Which it serves is not determined here: the pack is one pool, and this model carries no provenance from a charging interval to the discharge that empties it, so 53.5¢ describes the charging only. Super-off-peak imports at 12.5¢ stay unserved by the policy's rule; this comparison plays no part in that.

How good is the controller? Bounding greedy against the true optimum modeled

The price-aware policy above is a threshold rule with no proof of optimality. To find out how much value it leaves unclaimed, the true bill-minimizing dispatch was computed directly: a linear program that minimizes the exact monthly per-TOU-period NEM-netting bill itself (a naive price-arbitrage proxy can bill worse). The array already exports 60% of what it makes (9,964 kWh/yr): 63% of those exports leave in the 10am–2pm window, while the EV charged between midnight and 6am on 323 of 365 nights. Since the 2026 TOU change a midday exported kWh is worth 7.6–10.6¢. That is one cell of a six-cell map, and it does not price a year's exports.

Priced hour by hour at each hour's period and day type, an exported kWh on this profile is worth between 22.9¢, every export settled as surplus at the NEM 2.0 credit, and 25.7¢, every export cancelling an import inside its own month and TOU period. Actual settlement lands between the two, because a month's exports partly cancel imports and partly run to surplus. Neither end rests on the cheapest cell: 64.6% of the year's exported kWh leaves in super-off-peak, 29.5% in off-peak at 46–50¢, and 5.9% in on-peak at 56–85¢.

What one more kW of panels would earn is a separate question this report does not answer. Exports are what is left after the house has taken its share; they are not the shape of added production, so part of an added panel's output would displace an import instead of leaving the meter. A battery is the better use of the same sunlight: it monetizes midday surplus at 61–87¢ instead of exporting it, and adds backup value.

(Both ends price data/report_data.json's hour-of-day export profiles through analysis/rates.py. The NEM 2.0 growth cap matters more than the math. SDG&E's NEM tariff (Schedule NEM, Sheet 15, Special Condition 7(b), per Decision D.16-04-020) caps a NEM 2.0 system's growth at the greater of 10% of its capacity "at the time the customer receives the ATO letter" or 1 kW. For this array's 10.05 kW DC nameplate, that's ~1.0 kW (10% and the flat 1 kW floor land almost on top of each other at this size). Exceed it, and the addition (or the whole array, your choice under the tariff) moves onto today's Solar Billing Plan (NBT), where exports earn avoided-cost rates worth a fraction of retail (§13: real hourly rates, realized against this home's export shape, average ~4.7¢/kWh).

Your NEM 2.0 status is one of this home's most valuable energy assets, worth $2,103.58–$2,455.64/yr at today's rates (§13). The cap permits about a tenth more capacity; crossing it puts all of that at risk. The cap alone decides the expansion question, whatever a marginal kW turns out to be worth.

Repowering with higher-capacity panels: also no. That trade only makes sense for failing or badly shaded arrays. Yours produces 1,642 kWh/kW/yr with a stable inverter-vs-meter ratio and no degradation signal at year 6. Array health: 6-year degradation trend

PVOutput's size-normalized daily efficiency (kWh per kW installed) across full years 2021–2025 ran 4.749 → 4.839 → 4.245 → 4.528 → 4.501. A naïve fit reads ~1.3–1.8%/yr, but this metric isn't weather-normalized and the swing is dominated by weather (the wet 2023 El Niño winter sits mid-series and steepens the slope; 2025 generation is still within ~5% of 2021). A microinverter failure would show as an abrupt step-down or a dead channel, and none appears; a gradual slope is a different signature. Verdict: normal aging, best-estimate ~0.5–1.0%/yr, no failing component. The array is performing as expected for its age. (The sharp drop in exported kWh in 2025 reflects higher self-consumption/load; it is not a generation drop.)

An artifact-backed refit (analysis/gross_import_decomposition.py → data/gross_import_decomposition.json) confirms the naïve range from a reproducible script: OLS −1.76%/yr, first-to-last CAGR −1.33%/yr, and a Theil-Sen median slope −1.48%/yr. The tighter 0.5–1.0%/yr best estimate is a qualitative downward adjustment (the naïve metric isn't weather-normalized, so true degradation is presumably lower); no committed artifact here derives it independently. This repo's soiling record bounds how far that adjustment can be trusted: a single validated event (the 2024-08-12 cleaning, ~134 dry days, ~4.4 months, with no rain) produced an 11.8% production swing, more than double the entire naïve four-year change (−5.2%).

With no daily weather or production record before 2025, this repo cannot separate how much of the 2021–2025 swing is soiling/weather timing versus true panel aging, and the confounding cuts both ways: the same evidence that could make the naïve trend look worse than true degradation could just as easily mask a true degradation rate worse than the naïve trend shows, if later years happened to be sunnier or cleaner. One validated soiling swing large enough to explain the entire four-year naïve change by itself rules out treating either direction as bounded.

True degradation is not determined from data in this repo, and no stated range bounds it; the 0.5–1.0%/yr figure is the qualitative judgment described above, which no committed artifact here derives or confirms. Gross imports are climbing: consumption, not the array — checked against two different assumptions

Comparable early-summer bill periods show gross imports rising from 1,438 kWh (5/25/24–6/25/24, 32 days) to 1,934 kWh (5/29/26–6/26/26, 29 days), with net imports rising even faster (346 → 987 kWh). That pattern is consistent with a production loss, a consumption increase, or both. Decomposing the 496 kWh gross-import change with an hourly counterfactual (2026's measured whole-home CT load and meter-derived production) attributes essentially all of it to consumption.

The two bill periods differ in length (32 vs 29 days), so the counterfactual is built against a real 32-day 2026 reference window (May 25–June 25) instead of stretching the shorter period's hours to stand in for a longer one. The most direct assumption gives each year that reference window's hour-by-hour shape, scaled uniformly to match its bill exactly: consumption is pinned by each year's exact net-usage figure plus an estimated production level, and production is the one free parameter per year, solved for. Under it the split is +491 kWh consumption against +5 kWh production.

(The two years' production levels aren't reported as a year-over-year percentage here: the fitted values are tied to bill periods of different lengths in a way that doesn't reduce to a clean rate comparison. The +5 kWh production term above, how much of the change in gross imports traces to the production factor, is the well-defined and comparable quantity, and it is what the rest of this finding relies on.)

No 2024 hourly shape exists to confirm this assumption independently, so a second one, materially different and as defensible as the first, was tested with the identical decomposition method: each year's consumption difference from the reference window concentrated in EV-charging hours (per-session EV detection) instead of spread uniformly across every hour. That gives +470 kWh consumption against +26 kWh production. The ladder is not flat: its marginal falls to $289 per 100 W by the step that takes the floor down 1,000 W, mostly because each successive 100 W strips less measured energy than it asks for. Read it as a rate near this floor. It does not scale to any amount removed. analysis/quiet_night_floor.py → data/quiet_night_floor.json.

14 · Methodology, sources & caveats

In one sentence: absolute bills are anchored to the actual statements, savings are model deltas at 6/1/2026 rates, and each figure carries a confidence label with the few non-artifact-backed items named as such.

  • Grid data: SDG&E Green Button 15-min export (37,248 intervals, separate import/export channels); last 365 days analyzed.
  • Solar/load data: Enphase SAM 8760 hourly consumption (2025+2026 stitched), Enphase Site Energy Production daily report, PVOutput daily records: three production sources within ±2%, 0.9999 daily correlation; night-time residual of the derived series ≈ 0.
  • Rates: SDG&E Total Rates Tables eff. 6/1/2026 (EV-TOU-5, EV-TOU-2, TOU-DR1, TOU-DR2, TOU-DR-P, TOU-ELEC, DR); CEA Adopted Residential Rates eff. 6/1/2026 (Clean Impact); PCIA 2023 vintage $0.02828; WF-NBC/DWR-BC $0.00591; BSC $0.79343/day; Coastal baselines 9.0/9.2 kWh/day.
  • NEM 2.0: per-interval netting, exports at retail minus ~2.1¢ NBCs; cross-checked against monthly TOU-period netting (0.3% agreement; unarchived workpaper, not artifact-backed). Grandfathering unaffected by plan changes or storage additions.
  • Battery model (integrated engine): three dispatch policies simulated per 15-min interval (analysis/battery_dispatch_policies.py, results in data/battery_dispatch_policies.json): evening-only ($1,720/yr for 1 × PW3), two-window (+6–9am house load; $1,954), and price-aware (every non-super-off-peak import; $2,328; the published basis). In the 6.3% of intervals carrying both import and export, the engine serves a discharge-window import instead of banking ~8¢ surplus.

    The battery modifies the physical import/export series and the modified year is re-billed with the canonical engine (analysis/rates.py: monthly per-period NEM netting, NBC on gross imports), the same engine as the behavior model, so package figures come from one pipeline: EV shift first, then battery, re-billed end-to-end (MID battery marginal $2,238; no cross-model splicing).

    Charging is solar-surplus-first, then super-off-peak grid; 90% round-trip; 11.5 kW discharge for both configs, but different continuous charge caps per Tesla's official Powerwall 3 datasheet: 5 kW for the bare 1×PW3, 8 kW for PW3 + Expansion (an expansion pack re-rates the charge port, not the discharge rating). EV-spillover intervals (≥2.5 kW outside on-peak) are never battery-served, because the behavior fix moves that load for free. The §4 plan-matrix table is regenerated by analysis/battery_plan_matrix.py: the same price-aware dispatch trace billed under each top-3 plan's published table rates (data/battery_plan_matrix.json).

  • Behavior model: EV sessions detected as contiguous ≥30-min runs ≥2.5 kW above a rolling 24-h baseline with ≥8 kW peak excess; shifted energy physically added to same/next-day super-off-peak intervals under an 11.5 kW charger cap, then the modified year re-billed (energy conserved to the Wh; verified).
  • Package math: savings are deltas from analysis/behavior_rebuild.py: energy physically moved into destination super-off-peak intervals (11.5 kW charger cap respected, energy conserved to the Wh) and re-billed with the monthly per-TOU-period NEM netting model at current 6/1/2026 rates; absolute bills anchored to the $3,282 actual (§10). Paybacks are simple (no discounting, no rate escalation; SDG&E's recent high-single-digit annual increases would shorten them). No federal ITC (expired 2025); SGIP not assumed.
  • PCIA & NBC accounting (bill evidence): the same statement charges PCIA on 224 kWh (the period's net usage) while charging the wildfire fund on 308 kWh (gross imports), so the engine nets PCIA inside the energy rate and charges NBC on gross. Every month of the audit year was net-positive, so PCIA's treatment in a net-negative period is untested against a bill (flagged, not assumed). Constants and engine live in analysis/rates.py; billing_model_nem.py and behavior_rebuild.py import them (no re-declared rates).
  • Plan-matrix scope: the §3 and §4 cross-plan comparisons use published rate-table values, the sanctioned basis for ranking plans (bill-derived canonical rates exist only for the billed plan). The §4 battery×plan matrix is a simulation result: analysis/battery_plan_matrix.py bills the §6 price-aware PW3 dispatch under each top-3 plan's rates (data/battery_plan_matrix.json); its no-battery column ties out to data/plan_results.csv (asserted in-script) and its EV-TOU-5 battery value is cross-checked against the canonical engine ($2,318 table-rate vs $2,328 bill-derived; the rate basis accounts for the gap, and both use the canonical TOU calendar).
  • Bill validation: 12 detailed electric statements (13 billing periods; one statement splits at a rate change) parsed by analysis/parse_bills.py → data/electric_bill_summary.csv, covering the full 365 days; generation rates matched to the penny; CCA product confirmed "Clean Impact Plus" with no relief credit; climate zone confirmed Coastal; actual annual current charges $3,282 (§10). The parser reads periods from the statement text, never from filenames, and its per-bill TOU rates are committed in data/bill_tou_detail.csv.
  • Confidence labels: measured = read directly from meters, bills, or multi-source-verified records; modeled = computed with the validated billing/dispatch models at current rates; estimated = approximate inputs (rate history, bracketed soiling); treat as order-of-magnitude. Sections 1–10 are measured/modeled throughout; labels are shown where evidence is thinner.

  • TOU-spread escalation (§13): analysis/tou_spread.py → data/tou_spread.json, fitted on the per-bill printed rates in data/bill_tou_detail.csv, never on a rate table. Each observation is one rate segment dated at its midpoint (a period splits when a tariff changes mid-cycle), so a rate in force for a week is not weighted like one in force for a month. Rows priced at $0.00000 are excluded as NEM export-settlement deferrals, not prices; the script asserts that every such row carries kWh ≤ 0 and aborts if one does not, so a true zero price cannot be dropped silently.

    Adequacy rule, fixed before fitting: a cell needs ≥3 distinct rate vintages and ≥365 days. A spread claim needs all four of: both its cells adequate; the full-window 95% CI excludes zero; the refit on the window after the largest single step agrees in sign and is itself adequate; and that post-break CI still excludes zero after being widened for the fact that the breakpoint was chosen from the data. The last two decide the answer on this corpus: winter clears the first two bars and fails the fourth.

    Inference is fitted on distinct rate changes, never on statement prints, and on ln(spread), so the rate that gets compounded is the rate that was estimated. Each of these bars can only reject a spread claim. Delivery component only; the section gives the reason generation cannot join the series.

  • NBT counterfactual & price map (§13): same netting engine with exports at flat 3/5/8¢ (NBT-style) vs NEM 2.0 netting; price map is the bill-validated rate table itself (data/extra_results.json).
  • Phantom decomposition (§13): analysis/quiet_night_floor.py → data/quiet_night_floor.json. Per calendar night, the median 1–5am import power, excluding any night whose 1–5am maximum reaches 2 kW: a per-night gate, so one high-power interval disqualifies the whole night, and it names measured behavior, with the cause left open. Cycling = within-night std; seasonality = monthly medians of the kept nights. The floor is then priced two ways at rates drawn from analysis/rates.py (per-interval against the price map, and a full monthly NEM re-bill of the counterfactual year), with the measured floor held constant across all 8,760 hours; the two agree to 1.2%, and a 100 W sensitivity ladder re-bills the same year at each step.
