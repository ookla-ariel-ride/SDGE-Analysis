How the battery saves money

The model stores solar surplus only when its later value exceeds the export credit given up. It tops up from cheap grid power. It then serves eligible house demand during expensive hours, including the morning and late-evening shoulders.

High-power off-peak intervals are treated as EV charging in this model and excluded from battery service. The free schedule change moves that load first. The battery controls need to preserve this separation; otherwise the purchase estimate needs a new simulation.

Dispatch policy: price-aware saves more in the billing model modeled

A stored kWh costs 11.7¢ from midday solar surplus or 14.0¢ from a super-off-peak grid top-up. It can replace imports at 61–87¢ on-peak (4–9pm) or ~51¢ off-peak. Weekday off-peak hours are 6–10am, 2–4pm, and 9pm–midnight. The model re-bills each policy's full year because charging time affects stored-energy cost.

The price-aware policy tests both charging and discharge. It discharges against eligible imports outside super-off-peak. It accepts a charge only when a conservative estimate of delivered cost passes the price test. Declining costly charges adds $138/yr here. Average solar surplus of ~27 kWh/day does not establish the midday energy available; timing, price, and charge limits still matter.

analysis/rates.py prices the energy given up during the actual charging intervals. A midday export offsets an import within the same month and TOU period here. All 13 super-off-peak monthly buckets remain net importers because they include overnight EV charging. That makes the forgone export worth the netted rate (~10.5¢), rather than the 7.6¢ surplus export credit. Dividing by 90% round-trip efficiency gives 11.7¢ delivered.

NBC applies to the positive remainder of imports minus physical exports in each interval. Changed exports can therefore change NBC separately from monthly energy credits. §3 defines these charges; §14 gives the bill evidence and limits on the utility mechanics.

All 674.2 kWh stored from solar comes from midday super-off-peak surplus: 100.0%, at 11.7¢ delivered. Morning 6–10am and afternoon 2–4pm exports instead give up 46.2–49.8¢/kWh. After storage losses, that costs 51.3–55.3¢ delivered, above the 48.3–51.9¢ off-peak import it could replace. The policy exports that energy and preserves room for cheaper midday charging.

Summer off-peak buckets settle as net exports and supply the low end of those ranges. They account for 3.1% of declined surplus. Even at that lower credit, the same price test declines the charge, by a smaller margin.

The model records a delivered cost for each stored batch of energy. It discharges a batch only when the avoided import costs more at that decision. The year-wide check tests those comparisons. Later actions can change monthly settlement, so this does not guarantee a final profit on each batch. The policy leaves 12.5¢ super-off-peak imports alone: solar clears them by under a cent, while the 14.0¢ grid refill loses money on that round trip.

    The pre-fit adequacy rule needs at least three distinct rate vintages and 365 days per cell.
